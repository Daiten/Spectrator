Molecular Property Lab v23

A local, structure-based predictor for colour, odour, taste and bioactivity. Organic predictions are computed from structure with no molecule lookup; inorganic hues use a small explicit reference table of common ions and solids, because d-orbital splitting and band gaps cannot be derived from composition alone. Recognizes aromatic rings whether written in lowercase or Kekule form, and handles disconnected/salt structures.

How to interpret the taste profile

Taste is even less determined by a bare 2D graph than smell or color: it depends on exact stereochemistry, ionization state in saliva, and specific receptor geometry (the Shallenberger-Acree AH-B theory of sweetness, for instance, needs a precise 3D donor/acceptor distance this engine cannot see). Treat this as a rough structural prior, not a claim about how a real sample would taste.

Read this first. This tab flags recognized structural patterns — it is a teaching tool for structure–activity relationships, not a pharmacology or safety assessment. It cannot tell you whether anything is safe to take, at what dose, or how it will interact with a person or their medication. Scaffold matches say "this looks like a known family", never "this will work" or "this is safe". Real activity and real toxicity depend on stereochemistry, metabolism, dose and individual biology that a 2D structure simply does not contain. For anything involving an actual substance and an actual person, consult a qualified professional or a poison-control centre.

How to interpret this tab

Descriptors (MW, logP, TPSA, H-bond counts, rotatable bonds) are computed from the structure and are reasonably accurate. Everything above that — scaffold matching, receptor attribution, toxicity alerts — is pattern recognition against known families. A scaffold match means the molecule shares a shape with a known class; it does not establish binding, potency, efficacy or selectivity, all of which require actual experiments. Absence of an alert is not evidence of safety: many highly toxic compounds contain no classical alert at all, and many safe approved drugs do contain one.

How to interpret this tab

pKa values are class averages for each functional group, not predictions for the specific molecule: substituents routinely shift a real pKa by several units, so treat "weakly acidic" as a category rather than a number. Aqueous solubility for molecules uses the ESOL model (an empirical fit to logP, size, flexibility and aromatic surface) and is typically good to about an order of magnitude; ionic solids instead use the classical qualitative solubility rules, since a lattice is not what ESOL describes. Redox notes flag groups with well-known oxidising or reducing behaviour and say nothing about rate, potential, or what happens under any particular conditions.

Benchmark against your own dataset

Paste hand-checked reference data to score the model. Properties: color, odor, taste, acidbase, redox, solubility, psychoactive, toxic. Matching is keyword-based, so "sour" matches "sour/acidic" — you are scoring conclusions, not wording. Lines beginning with # are ignored, and any row the parser cannot read is reported rather than silently dropped.

Three layouts are accepted, so a compound need only be written once:

One claim per row
COc1ccccc1O,odor,smoky

Several properties on one row — repeat property/value pairs after the input
COc1ccccc1O,odor,smoky,taste,bitter,color,colorless

A column per property — needs a header row naming an input (or smiles/compound) column; an optional name column is carried into the failure list, and blank cells are skipped
input,name,odor,taste,color
COc1ccccc1O,guaiacol,smoky,bitter,colorless

How to use this well

Collect discrepancies in batches of twenty or more before acting on them: nearly every real bug found in this project affected a whole class of compounds, and a batch makes the pattern visible where a single case invites an over-fitted patch. Include compounds the model gets right as well — knowing that eight esters pass and two fail is far more diagnostic than two failures alone. Prefer pairs that differ in one feature (hexanal against octanal, anthraquinone against its amino derivative) since those locate a boundary, and be wary of tuning until this number rises: an accuracy figure that only improves on the rows in this box is measuring memorisation, not prediction.

What can and cannot be predicted here

For a simple binary ionic solid the radius-ratio rule is a real structural predictor: the ratio of ionic radii sets the coordination number, and that plus the stoichiometry gives the structure type. It is right for perhaps two thirds of cases on its own, and two well-known systematic failures are corrected explicitly — soft polarisable cations with heavy chalcogenides go tetrahedral through covalency, and the caesium chloride structure is far rarer than the classical cut-off implies.

For a molecular (organic) crystal no honest prediction is possible from a connection table. Packing is decided by three-dimensional shape and intermolecular contacts that a 2D structure does not contain; dedicated crystal-structure-prediction codes with very large compute budgets still fail regularly, and polymorphism means many compounds have several correct answers at once. What this panel gives instead is a hard symmetry constraint where one exists — an enantiopure chiral molecule cannot adopt a centrosymmetric space group, since an inversion centre would demand the opposite enantiomer — together with base rates over known structures, clearly labelled as base rates.
